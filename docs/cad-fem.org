#+TITLE: The CAD kernel and the finite-element solver
#+OPTIONS: toc:2 num:nil

* Why it is here at all

mep has a boundary-representation CAD kernel and a finite-element solver
in it, written from scratch, with no OpenCASCADE, no CalculiX and no
Eigen. The reason is the same one behind the PDF stack, the browser
engine and the language servers: a dependency is a thing you cannot fix,
cannot verify and cannot teach. What makes this particular one worth the
effort is that the two halves are only interesting *together*.

A CAD kernel on its own is a modeller. A finite-element solver on its own
is a thing you feed a mesh. Keeping the exact geometry all the way
through -- modelling, meshing, binding conditions, solving, estimating
the error, and then meshing again -- is what lets the last of those
happen at all. A program that imports a mesh can subdivide the elements
it was given; it cannot put new nodes on the fillet, because it no longer
has the fillet. It has a faceted approximation of one, and every
refinement makes a finer approximation of the facets rather than of the
part.

* The shape of it

** Geometry

~src/cad_math~, ~cad_nurbs~, ~cad_curve~ and ~cad_surface~ are the
analytic layer: points, curves, surfaces and their derivatives, with
analytic forms kept analytic. A cylinder is a =CylinderSurface=, not a
B-spline that happens to be round, because the operations downstream --
filleting, drafting, and the STEP writer -- depend on knowing which it
is.

~cad_topology~ is the B-rep proper: vertices, edges, coedges, loops,
faces, shells, bodies. ~cad_predicates~ underneath it is exact
arithmetic, because a boolean that decides "on which side of this plane"
with floating point decides it inconsistently for two faces that share an
edge, and an inconsistent answer is a solid with a hole in it.

~cad_intersect~, ~cad_classify~, ~cad_arrangement~ and ~cad_boolean~ are
the boolean operator. ~cad_feature~, ~cad_modify~, ~cad_pattern~ and
~cad_assembly~ are what a user actually reaches for. ~cad_naming~ is the
persistent naming that makes a reference to "that face" survive a
rebuild, which is what a parametric feature tree is *for* and is the part
most kernels get least right.

** Analysis

~fem_mesh_*~ is the mesher: a background sizing field over an octree,
then a surface mesh, then a Delaunay tetrahedralisation, then quality
improvement, then optionally curved second-order elements whose mid-side
nodes are projected onto the real surface -- which is the concrete payoff
of still having the real surface.

~fem_elem~ is the element library, eleven types, each held to properties
that follow from what an element *is* rather than to a known answer.
~fem_study~ binds conditions expressed against the geometry onto the
mesh; ~fem_assemble~ builds the system; ~num_sparse~ factorises or
iterates on it.

~fem_static~, ~fem_thermal~, ~fem_modal~, ~fem_nonlinear~, ~fem_plastic~
and ~fem_contact~ are the physics. ~fem_result~, ~fem_render~,
~fem_probe~, ~fem_animate~, ~fem_movie~ and ~fem_adapt~ are what turns a
solved system back into something a person can read, and the last of
those closes the loop back to the geometry.

~fem_movie~ is a software rasteriser -- z-buffer, perspective-correct
interpolation, two-sided shading, its own small typeface for the colour
bar -- feeding the same JPEG encoder and QuickTime muxer the 3D
modeller's export uses. It is not routed through the modeller's own
renderer for two reasons: that one animates *transforms*, and a mode
shape is not a transform of anything, since every node moves
differently; and it needs an OpenGL context, while a benchmark that
produces its own film has to run in a test on a machine with no display.
A rasteriser with no dependencies also renders the same picture
everywhere, which is what lets a test assert what is in one.

* How it is verified

Nothing here is checked by comparing against itself. Each part is held to
one of four things:

1. *A closed form.* A block's volume, a cantilever's deflection, the area
   of a sphere, Euler's buckling load, the slope of a bar past yield.
2. *A convergence rate.* An element's error must fall at the order its
   own theory predicts -- squared in h for a linear element, cubed for a
   quadratic one. A rate is far harder to fake than a number: an element
   that is wrong converges at the wrong rate, or to the wrong thing.
3. *A property that follows from what the thing is.* Shape functions sum
   to one; a stiffness matrix has exactly six zero eigenvalues; a closed
   surface encloses a known volume; a mode shape is orthogonal to the
   others; strain energy rises under refinement and never falls.
4. *Another program entirely.* Gmsh for meshes, CalculiX for solves,
   pdftoppm-style image comparison for renders. These are opt-in recipes
   in the justfile rather than test dependencies.

The one that has caught the most real bugs is the third. A residual at
round-off proves a system was solved, not that it was the right system,
and almost every mistake in this area produces a solver that runs,
converges, and is wrong by ten percent.

* The agent surface

Part K's rule is *one implementation and three adapters*. The methods
live in one table (~src/cad_fem_methods.cpp~) with one dispatch
(~src/cad_fem_api.cpp~), and the Lua bindings, the agent-RPC dispatch,
the MCP tool declarations and the reference documentation are each a loop
over that table. The alternative -- four hand-maintained lists -- does
not stay in step, and the test asserts that this one does: every declared
method must be implemented, and the checked-in documentation must be what
the generator would write.

The method table is deliberately in its own translation unit with no
kernel dependency, so ~mep-mcp~ -- which is a plain socket client and
links none of mep_core -- can declare thirty CAD and FEM tools without
linking a B-rep kernel into a process whose job is forwarding JSON.

* The examples

~examples/nafems/~ is nine NAFEMS benchmarks as parts you can open --
one ~.mepcad~ feature tree each, and one Lua script that opens it, states
the benchmark's own conditions, solves, compares against a reference and
writes a film. They use *only* the agent surface above, which is the
point: ~src/fem_nafems_test.cpp~ builds its meshes in C++ and proves the
solver, and these prove that the surface can express the same problems.

It was meant to be a demonstration and turned into a test. It found five
defects -- a pressure acting the wrong way on a swept mesh, a kernel
tolerance meant for millimetres applied to a part in metres, a face
listing whose reported point is not on the face, two missing field names
-- and three capabilities that existed but were unreachable. Four of the
nine are still blocked, three in the mesher and one in the kernel.
~plans/NAFEMS_PLAN.md~ has the whole account.

* What is not here

~plans/CAD_FEM_PLAN.md~ records this per part, in detail, as unchecked
boxes under each finished item. The largest gaps at the time of writing:
the mesher is scale dependent and grades poorly, which is what stops the
adaptive loop after two or three cycles; there is no mortar contact, no
friction and no self-contact; hyperelasticity is named and not written;
and nothing here is wired into the editor's panes yet -- the drawing
routines return arrays, and no pane assembles them.
